# Matching Score Evaluation Template

This document provides detailed rubrics and guidelines for the 3D matching score evaluation system used in SSCI journal selection.

## Overview

The 3D matching score system evaluates journal suitability across three dimensions:
- **Topic Fit** (0-5): Alignment with journal's thematic focus
- **Method Fit** (0-5): Compatibility with journal's methodological preferences
- **Audience Fit** (0-5): Match with journal's target readership

**Total Score Range**: 0-15 points
**Evidence Level**: E0-E3 (based on research depth)
**Desk Reject Risk**: High/Medium/Low

## Topic Fit Scoring Rubric (0-5)

### 0 Points: Completely Mismatched
**Definition**: Journal explicitly excludes your research area or shows no evidence of publishing related work.

**Indicators**:
- Scope statement explicitly mentions topics that exclude your research
- No articles in your field published in last 3-5 years
- Journal focuses on completely different discipline
- Recent special issues/themes show no overlap with your topic

**Examples**:
- Your research: Educational technology in K-12
- Journal scope: "Pure theoretical mathematics"
- Recent articles: All about abstract algebra, no education content

**Evidence Required**: Direct quotes from scope + analysis of recent article titles

### 1 Point: Minimal Relevance
**Definition**: Very weak connection; journal occasionally touches on related areas but not a focus.

**Indicators**:
- Scope mentions broad categories that might include your topic
- 1-2 articles in vaguely related area in last 3 years
- Your topic appears as minor aspect of broader studies
- No dedicated focus on your specific research problem

**Examples**:
- Your research: Social media impact on teen mental health
- Journal scope: "General psychology and behavior"
- Recent articles: Mostly clinical psychology, one article mentions "digital behavior" tangentially

**Evidence Required**: Scope analysis + 3-5 recent article titles showing minimal connection

### 2 Points: Some Relevance
**Definition**: Journal publishes in related areas but your specific topic is not a priority.

**Indicators**:
- Scope includes your general field but emphasizes other sub-topics
- Occasional articles (2-3 per year) in related areas
- Your specific research question is not addressed
- Methodological or population differences exist

**Examples**:
- Your research: AI ethics in healthcare
- Journal scope: "Technology and society" with emphasis on policy
- Recent articles: Tech policy, digital governance, one article on "medical technology" but not AI ethics

**Evidence Required**: Scope analysis + 5-8 recent articles showing some thematic overlap

### 3 Points: Moderate Fit
**Definition**: Journal regularly publishes in your general area; some direct relevance to your topic.

**Indicators**:
- Scope explicitly mentions your research area or closely related topics
- 3-5 articles per year in your general field
- Some articles address similar research questions or populations
- Your specific contribution may be novel but fits within journal's interests

**Examples**:
- Your research: Online learning effectiveness in STEM education
- Journal scope: "Educational technology and digital learning"
- Recent articles: MOOC effectiveness, digital pedagogy, one article on "STEM online courses"

**Evidence Required**: Scope quotes + 8-12 recent articles with clear thematic connections

### 4 Points: Strong Fit
**Definition**: Journal frequently publishes work very similar to yours; clear alignment.

**Indicators**:
- Scope specifically mentions your research area as a focus
- 5+ articles per year directly related to your topic
- Recent articles address similar questions, methods, or populations
- Your work extends or complements existing journal content
- Recent special issues or calls for papers in your area

**Examples**:
- Your research: Gamification in language learning
- Journal scope: "Digital learning environments and educational games"
- Recent articles: Multiple articles on gamification, serious games in education, language learning technologies

**Evidence Required**: Scope quotes + 10-15 recent articles with strong thematic alignment

### 5 Points: Perfect Fit
**Definition**: Journal is a leading venue for your specific research area; exceptional alignment.

**Indicators**:
- Scope explicitly prioritizes your research area
- Journal is considered a top venue in your specific field
- Multiple recent articles on your exact topic or very similar work
- Recent special issues, editorials, or calls for papers targeting your research
- Your work directly continues conversations in this journal
- Editorial board includes experts in your specific area

**Examples**:
- Your research: VR-based language learning for medical professionals
- Journal scope: "Virtual reality in specialized language education"
- Recent articles: Multiple VR language learning studies, medical education focus, editorial on "emerging technologies in professional language training"

**Evidence Required**: Comprehensive scope analysis + 15+ recent articles + evidence of editorial priorities

## Method Fit Scoring Rubric (0-5)

### 0 Points: Methodologically Incompatible
**Definition**: Journal explicitly rejects your methodology or shows no evidence of publishing similar methods.

**Indicators**:
- Submission guidelines explicitly exclude your method type
- No articles using your methodology in last 3-5 years
- Journal has clear methodological bias against your approach
- Recent editorials criticize methods similar to yours

**Examples**:
- Your method: Qualitative case study
- Journal guidelines: "We only publish quantitative empirical research"
- Recent articles: All statistical analyses, no qualitative work

**Evidence Required**: Direct quotes from guidelines + methodological analysis of recent articles

### 1 Point: Methodologically Marginal
**Definition**: Your method appears occasionally but is not preferred or well-represented.

**Indicators**:
- Guidelines don't mention your method type
- 1-2 articles using your method in last 3 years
- Mixed methodological preferences with emphasis on other approaches
- Your method appears as secondary or supplementary

**Examples**:
- Your method: Mixed methods design
- Journal emphasis: Primarily quantitative
- Recent articles: Mostly surveys and experiments, one mixed-methods article as exception

**Evidence Required**: Guidelines analysis + 3-5 recent articles showing methodological patterns

### 2 Points: Methodologically Acceptable
**Definition**: Journal publishes your method type but with some limitations or preferences.

**Indicators**:
- Guidelines mention your method as acceptable
- Occasional articles (2-3 per year) using your method
- Some methodological preferences evident (e.g., sample size, design type)
- Your specific methodological approach may need justification

**Examples**:
- Your method: Longitudinal survey design
- Journal: Accepts surveys but prefers cross-sectional
- Recent articles: Mix of designs, some methodological discussions about longitudinal challenges

**Evidence Required**: Guidelines review + 5-8 recent articles with methodological analysis

### 3 Points: Methodologically Compatible
**Definition**: Journal regularly publishes your method type; good fit with some preferences.

**Indicators**:
- Guidelines explicitly mention your method as welcome
- 3-5 articles per year using your method
- Clear methodological preferences that align with your approach
- Recent methodological discussions support your design choices
- Your specific methodological innovations are within journal's comfort zone

**Examples**:
- Your method: Ethnographic fieldwork
- Journal: Strong qualitative tradition
- Recent articles: Multiple ethnographies, methodological articles on qualitative approaches

**Evidence Required**: Guidelines quotes + 8-12 recent articles with methodological alignment

### 4 Points: Methodologically Strong
**Definition**: Journal frequently publishes your method type; excellent fit with preferences.

**Indicators**:
- Guidelines specifically encourage your method type
- 5+ articles per year using your method
- Methodological innovations in your area are welcomed
- Recent special issues on methodological advances in your field
- Editorial board includes methodological experts in your approach
- Your methodological contributions align with journal's innovation priorities

**Examples**:
- Your method: Experimental design with neuroimaging
- Journal: Leading experimental psychology journal
- Recent articles: Multiple neuroimaging studies, methodological advances in experimental design

**Evidence Required**: Guidelines analysis + 10-15 recent articles + evidence of methodological leadership

### 5 Points: Methodologically Ideal
**Definition**: Journal is a premier venue for your specific methodology; perfect alignment.

**Indicators**:
- Journal is known for methodological innovation in your area
- Multiple recent articles using your exact method or very similar approaches
- Recent methodological debates, special issues, or editorials focus on your method
- Your methodological contributions directly advance conversations in this journal
- Editorial board includes leading experts in your methodological approach
- Journal actively seeks methodological innovations in your field

**Examples**:
- Your method: Advanced machine learning for educational data mining
- Journal: Leading computational education journal
- Recent articles: Multiple ML applications in education, special issue on "AI in learning analytics"

**Evidence Required**: Comprehensive methodological analysis + 15+ recent articles + evidence of methodological leadership

## Audience Fit Scoring Rubric (0-5)

### 0 Points: Audience Mismatch
**Definition**: Journal's target readership has no interest in or use for your research.

**Indicators**:
- Journal explicitly targets different audience (practitioners vs. researchers, different fields)
- No evidence that your research questions would interest the readership
- Journal's mission and scope don't align with your research implications
- Recent articles show completely different audience priorities

**Examples**:
- Your research: Theoretical framework for online learning
- Journal audience: K-12 classroom teachers
- Recent articles: Practical teaching tips, classroom management

**Evidence Required**: Audience analysis from journal materials + content analysis of recent articles

### 1 Point: Limited Audience Interest
**Definition**: Small subset of readership might find your work marginally interesting.

**Indicators**:
- Journal serves broad audience with diverse interests
- Your specific research area is not a priority for most readers
- Some tangential connection to audience interests
- Your work would be of peripheral rather than central interest

**Examples**:
- Your research: Advanced statistical methods for learning analytics
- Journal audience: General education researchers
- Recent articles: Mostly applied research with basic statistics

**Evidence Required**: Audience description + analysis of article topics and their relevance

### 2 Points: Moderate Audience Relevance
**Definition**: Significant portion of readership would find your work relevant but not central.

**Indicators**:
- Journal serves audience with varied research interests
- Your work addresses topics that interest some reader subgroups
- Your research has some practical or theoretical implications for the field
- Some recent articles show similar audience engagement patterns

**Examples**:
- Your research: Cross-cultural comparison of MOOC completion rates
- Journal audience: International education researchers
- Recent articles: Mix of cultural studies and online learning research

**Evidence Required**: Audience analysis + content analysis showing relevance patterns

### 3 Points: Good Audience Fit
**Definition**: Majority of readership would find your work directly relevant and valuable.

**Indicators**:
- Journal's target audience aligns well with your research area
- Your work addresses questions important to the readership
- Clear practical or theoretical implications for the audience
- Recent articles show similar patterns of audience engagement
- Your research contributes to ongoing conversations in the field

**Examples**:
- Your research: Teacher professional development for technology integration
- Journal audience: Educational technology researchers and teacher educators
- Recent articles: Multiple studies on teacher training and technology adoption

**Evidence Required**: Audience description + analysis of 8-12 recent articles showing engagement patterns

### 4 Points: Strong Audience Alignment
**Definition**: Journal's core readership would highly value and actively use your research.

**Indicators**:
- Journal specifically targets audience that needs your type of research
- Your work addresses pressing questions or needs of the readership
- Strong practical applications or theoretical contributions for the audience
- Recent articles, editorials, or special issues show similar high-value research
- Your research advances the field in ways the audience actively seeks

**Examples**:
- Your research: Evidence-based strategies for inclusive online learning design
- Journal audience: Learning designers and accessibility specialists
- Recent articles: Multiple inclusive design studies, special issue on accessibility

**Evidence Required**: Comprehensive audience analysis + 10-15 recent articles + evidence of audience needs

### 5 Points: Perfect Audience Match
**Definition**: Journal's primary audience considers your research area essential and urgently needed.

**Indicators**:
- Journal exists specifically to serve audience interested in your research
- Your work addresses critical gaps or emerging needs of the readership
- Exceptional practical value or theoretical advancement for the audience
- Recent calls for papers, editorials, or special issues target your research area
- Your research directly supports the audience's core mission or professional needs
- Journal actively seeks to publish work like yours

**Examples**:
- Your research: Crisis-responsive distance learning frameworks for higher education
- Journal audience: Higher education emergency response planners
- Recent articles: Multiple crisis learning studies, editorial on "preparing for future disruptions"

**Evidence Required**: Deep audience understanding + 15+ recent articles + evidence of audience priorities

## Evidence Level Definitions (E0-E3)

### E0: Almost No Evidence
**Definition**: Minimal research done; evaluation based on superficial information.

**What's Available**:
- Journal name and basic description
- Impact factor or basic metrics
- General disciplinary classification
- No detailed scope analysis
- No review of recent articles

**When to Use**:
- Initial screening of many journals
- Preliminary list building
- Quick elimination of obvious mismatches

**Limitations**:
- High risk of inaccurate assessment
- Cannot make confident recommendations
- Should not be used for final decisions

**Upgrade Path**: Move to E1 by reading scope statement and basic metrics

### E1: Basic Information + Scope Level
**Definition**: Basic research completed; evaluation based on available metadata and scope.

**What's Available**:
- Detailed scope statement analysis
- Impact factor, quartile, and basic metrics
- Publisher and indexing information
- General methodological preferences
- Basic OA and APC information
- 5-10 recent article titles reviewed

**When to Use**:
- Narrowing candidate pool from many to few
- Initial assessment of promising journals
- Deciding which journals deserve deeper investigation

**Limitations**:
- Still limited understanding of actual content
- Cannot assess methodological fit in detail
- Risk of missing important nuances

**Upgrade Path**: Move to E2 by reviewing 10-20 recent articles in detail

### E2: Recent Article Level
**Definition**: Substantial research completed; evaluation based on detailed article analysis.

**What's Available**:
- Comprehensive scope and aims analysis
- 10-20 recent articles reviewed in detail
- Understanding of methodological preferences and patterns
- Clear sense of thematic priorities and gaps
- Evidence of audience engagement and impact
- Recent special issues and editorial priorities

**When to Use**:
- Final evaluation of top candidate journals
- Making submission decisions
- Detailed matching score assessment
- Evidence-based recommendations

**Limitations**:
- May miss very recent shifts in editorial direction
- Limited understanding of reviewer expectations
- Cannot predict desk reject risk with certainty

**Upgrade Path**: Move to E3 by reading high-impact articles and editorial materials

### E3: Deep Understanding Level
**Definition**: Comprehensive research completed; evaluation based on deep content knowledge.

**What's Available**:
- All E2 level information
- 1-2 high-impact articles read in detail
- Recent editorial materials and guidance articles
- Understanding of reviewer expectations and standards
- Knowledge of editorial board expertise and interests
- Awareness of recent field developments and priorities
- Understanding of journal's role in scholarly conversations

**When to Use**:
- Final submission decisions for top journals
- High-stakes publication decisions
- Strategic long-term planning
- Mentoring others in journal selection

**Limitations**:
- Time-intensive to achieve
- May be overkill for lower-tier journals
- Still cannot guarantee acceptance

## Desk Reject Risk Assessment

### High Risk Indicators
**Definition**: Strong likelihood of immediate rejection without peer review.

**Scope-Related Indicators**:
- Explicit exclusion of your topic area in scope statement
- Clear emphasis on different research priorities
- Recent editorials criticizing approaches similar to yours
- Special issues showing different thematic focus

**Method-Related Indicators**:
- Submission guidelines explicitly exclude your methodology
- Recent articles show strong methodological bias against your approach
- Editorial statements about methodological preferences
- Your method requires extensive justification in cover letter

**Audience-Related Indicators**:
- Clear mismatch between your research and journal's target readership
- Recent articles show different audience engagement patterns
- Your work would be of minimal interest to the readership

**Structural Indicators**:
- Article type or format doesn't match journal conventions
- Length or structure significantly different from typical articles
- Your work requires major restructuring to fit journal format

**When to Choose High Risk**: 2+ strong indicators from different categories

### Medium Risk Indicators
**Definition**: Possible desk reject depending on presentation and justification.

**Scope-Related Indicators**:
- Your topic is on the edge of journal's scope
- Some ambiguity in scope interpretation
- Recent shifts in editorial direction
- Your work is innovative but not clearly outside scope

**Method-Related Indicators**:
- Your method is acceptable but not preferred
- Some methodological concerns that need addressing
- Your approach requires careful justification
- Recent methodological debates in the field

**Audience-Related Indicators**:
- Some reader subgroups would value your work more than others
- Your implications are not immediately obvious to the audience
- Need to clearly articulate relevance in cover letter

**Structural Indicators**:
- Minor formatting or structural adjustments needed
- Your contribution needs clear articulation
- Some elements don't perfectly align with conventions

**When to Choose Medium Risk**: 1-2 indicators, or several weak indicators

### Low Risk Indicators
**Definition**: Very unlikely to be desk rejected; likely to receive peer review.

**Scope-Related Indicators**:
- Clear alignment with journal's stated scope and priorities
- Recent articles show similar thematic focus
- Your work addresses questions the journal actively seeks
- Recent special issues or calls for papers in your area

**Method-Related Indicators**:
- Your methodology is well-established in the journal
- Recent articles use similar or identical methods
- Your methodological approach is within journal's comfort zone
- Clear evidence of methodological compatibility

**Audience-Related Indicators**:
- Strong alignment with journal's target readership
- Your work addresses questions important to the audience
- Recent articles show similar audience engagement patterns
- Clear practical or theoretical value for readers

**Structural Indicators**:
- Your article structure matches journal conventions
- Length and format are appropriate
- Your contribution is clearly articulated and justified

**When to Choose Low Risk**: Clear alignment across all categories with strong evidence

## Scoring Guidelines and Best Practices

### Evidence Requirements
- **0-1 Points**: Minimal evidence required (scope statement + 1-2 articles)
- **2-3 Points**: Moderate evidence required (scope + 5-10 articles)
- **4-5 Points**: Strong evidence required (scope + 10-20+ articles)

### Documentation Standards
Always document your evidence:
- Quote relevant scope statements
- List specific article titles and years
- Note methodological patterns observed
- Record audience analysis findings
- Cite recent special issues or editorial priorities

### Scoring Consistency
- Use the same evidence standards for all journals
- Compare journals within the same evaluation session
- Document your reasoning for each score
- Review and adjust scores after completing all evaluations

### Risk Mitigation
- Never rely solely on scores for final decisions
- Always consider the interaction between dimensions
- Factor in your personal constraints and risk tolerance
- Have backup options for high-risk choices
- Get second opinions on borderline cases

### Common Scoring Pitfalls
- **Over-scoring**: Giving high scores based on hope rather than evidence
- **Under-scoring**: Being too conservative with good matches
- **Scope bias**: Over-emphasizing scope at the expense of method or audience fit
- **Recency bias**: Over-weighting very recent articles
- **Impact factor bias**: Letting metrics override actual fit

This template provides the detailed framework needed to make objective, evidence-based journal selection decisions. Remember that scores are tools to support judgment, not replace it.
